# MIT AI Risks and Imperium Assessment

## Date

2026-07-23

## Question

Is Imperium addressing the 24 risks evaluated by the MIT AI Risk Initiative, and what is missing?

## Scope Qualification

Imperium is aimed directly at several risks created when organizations deploy autonomous or semi-autonomous agents.

Most relevant protection currently exists as architecture, doctrine, admitted semantics, or theoretical evidence rather than implemented enforcement. Track A changed no production implementation. B1.1–B1.3 have since established bounded provider-neutral deployment-policy evidence, but no provider, credential store, Runtime driver, deployment mechanism, Runtime enforcement, external crossing, or deployment has been selected or performed.

Therefore:

```text
architectural relevance ≠ implemented protection
```

## Aggregate Assessment

| Area | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Organizational agentic-governance relevance | High |
| Potential deployer-level risk coverage | High |
| Socioeconomic and geopolitical coverage | Low |
| Current Runtime-enforced protection | Low |
| Strongest strategic match | Governance failure, agency, authorization, procedural transparency, multi-agent accountability |
| Most serious conceptual omission | Affected-stakeholder representation and recourse |
| Most serious technical omission | Action-level enforcement, containment, revocation, and continuous assurance |

## Strong Alignments

Imperium provides relevant locations and distinctions for:

- explicit authority instead of inferred permission;
- separation among persona, recruitment, activation, deployment, tools, credentials, and decision authority;
- evidence and Provenance before decisions;
- human institutional authority above model initiative;
- quarantine of returning field information;
- mission-specific operatives rather than universally empowered agents;
- accountable multi-agent roles rather than an unstructured swarm;
- denial, constraint, contest, supersession, inspection, and recording.

## Material Limits

Imperium cannot by itself solve:

- market and state power centralization;
- international competitive dynamics;
- labor displacement and employment quality;
- environmental infrastructure cost;
- cultural devaluation of human work;
- society-scale disinformation or consensus fragmentation;
- model-developer failures outside its deployer authority.

## Cross-Cutting Missing Programs

1. `ICP-01` — Risk function.
2. `ICP-02` — Affected-stakeholder representation and recourse.
3. `ICP-03` — Enforceable deployment authorization.
4. `ICP-04` — Capability governance.
5. `ICP-05` — Continuous assurance.
6. `ICP-06` — Quantitative evidence.
7. `ICP-07` — External accountability.

## Conclusion

Imperium is not a complete answer to the MIT risk landscape.

It is unusually well aimed at the deployer-level question:

> How does an organization prevent capable AI operatives from acquiring implicit authority, uncontrolled tools, untraceable decision power, or permanent trust?

That is a meaningful governance layer. Its protection becomes real only when the relevant controls are implemented, verified, continuously reassessed, and open to affected-stakeholder and external accountability.
